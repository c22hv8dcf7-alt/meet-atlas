Our Story

Built because
renting your own mind got old

One researcher. One small laptop. One library that deserved better.

In 2026, every AI assistant lived in someone else’s building. You paid rent in subscriptions and sign-ups, your questions travelled to a data centre, and the thing that knew your work best belonged to a company you’d never met.

Atlas began that fall in Pennsylvania, with one researcher and one small laptop — an 8 GB MacBook Air, the kind of machine people actually own — and a library of real research that deserved better than being pasted into a stranger’s server. So he built his own assistant, on his own machine, out of open models anyone can download.

The first versions were rough. Then it learned the things that matter: to remember in plain files its owner can read and edit. To answer from the library it was given — with the source attached — or to say “I don’t know” out loud. To check its own work, layer by layer, and show its test scores. To keep talking with the Wi-Fi off — at a desk, in the field, for a whole week at a cabin.

That honesty has a field-science root: in a creek, the creatures living in the water are the report card, and they can’t be coached. Atlas was built to that standard. It shows its sources, or it says nothing.

Why the name? An atlas is the book of maps you own. It doesn’t phone home, it doesn’t expire, and nobody can take it off your shelf. Ours just happens to talk.

People who saw it asked the same question: can I have one? So it was rebuilt clean — everything personal taken out, everything honest kept in — and given away. Atlas is free, and it stays free. If it earns its keep, there’s a support page for the people who’d like to help keep it that way.

An independent project

Meet Atlas is built and maintained by one developer in Pennsylvania and published by Porch Light. Its story is its own — Atlas is not a game, not a startup play, and not part of a platform.

The timeline

Eight chapters, all of them true

  • Fall 2026

    The first build

    One researcher in Pennsylvania makes a decision: stop renting intelligence. The first private Atlas runs local open models on an 8 GB MacBook Air — the kind of machine people actually own — and answers questions from a personal research library, with the source attached to every answer. No server, no subscription, no audience. Just proof that the idea works on ordinary hardware.

  • Oct 2026

    A memory you can read — and a voice

    Atlas learns to remember, and it remembers the way a person owns a notebook: plain files on the disk that its owner can open, read, edit, or delete at any time. Voice arrives with it — talk to Atlas, hear it answer back — and so does the hardest feature of all: when the answer isn’t in the library, Atlas says “I don’t know” out loud instead of inventing something that sounds right.

  • Late Oct 2026

    The cabin test

    The honest exam: a full week at a cabin with the Wi-Fi off. Chat, voice, memory, the library — everything Atlas is — keeps working with no network at all, because none of it ever lived anywhere else. The cloud accounts Atlas replaced stay closed. That week is the standard every public claim on this site is held to: if it didn’t work offline, it doesn’t get said here.

  • Nov 2026

    Self-Check and the Evaluation

    Atlas learns to grade itself. Self-Check tests every layer — brain, voice, memory, web — separately, so one failure never takes the whole assistant down, and the Mechanic makes only small, safe, reversible repairs, saying what it did. Its Evaluation page — inside Atlas — puts the scores on show. An assistant that shows its report card, cites its sources, and admits what it doesn’t know: that’s the product.

  • Nov 4, 2026

    The public edition, announced

    People kept asking the same question — can I have one? — so Atlas was rebuilt clean: everything personal taken out, everything honest kept in. The Meet Atlas channel launches, this site opens, and the public edition is announced the way it will stay: free forever, no account, no subscription, Wi-Fi optional.

  • Nov 2026

    The Fit Check — one Atlas, sized to the machine

    Every other assistant ships one fixed size and hopes your computer keeps up. Atlas learns to size itself instead: at first run, its Fit Check — run by the engine we call the Governor — reads the machine it has landed on and picks the brain that actually fits — a 6 GB floor, with light machines getting the light brain and strong machines getting the strong one. One Atlas for every desk, instead of one stick for every desk. It was built and test-proven before it was allowed anywhere near a download.

  • Nov 4, 2026

    The first download

    The same day this site opened, the first public build shipped: Atlas 0.1.0 for Linux, free on the release page. Linux went first not because it was the plan’s order, but because it was the build that could be proven first — and on this site, proven beats promised. Windows follows on the same shared core, and the Mac public edition is in the works.

  • Roadmap

    The road ahead

    The Windows edition is next, on the same shared core, with the Mac public edition in the works — each announced only when it’s real. Free traveling Packs — a Student Pack, a Researcher Pack, an Offline Reference Pack — so a library assembled once can be carried anywhere Atlas runs. And a $0 reviewer program: the “review unit” is a free download, seeded to the local-AI reviewers who want to test it in the open. If the downloads ever ask for more, we’ll build more — in public, on this page.
