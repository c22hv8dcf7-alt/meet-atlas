Local AI, explained

AI that works without the internet

What local AI is, how it works, what machine it needs — and what it honestly can't do.

What "local AI" means

Most assistants live in a datacentre: you type, your words travel to a server farm, and an answer travels back — along with a monthly bill and a copy of your question kept on someone else's machine. Local AI runs on the computer already on your desk. Atlas is an AI that lives on your computer — not in someone else's cloud. Its brain, voice, memory and your library all live on your machine, and answers come from your own documents, with the source attached — or an honest "I don't know."

Three steps, no cloud in any of them

1 · Download. Free, with no account. A plain-words setup wizard sizes Atlas to the machine you have.

2 · Ask. Talk or type. Answers come from your own library, with the source attached — or an honest "I don't know."

3 · It learns. Atlas remembers in plain files you own, checks its own work, and keeps going with the Wi-Fi off.

The cabin test

This isn't a slogan; it's a test Atlas was put through: a full week, Wi-Fi off, everything working. Atlas ships with 0 telemetry and makes 0 background network requests. Web research exists, but it is an optional feature with its own switch — turn it off and Atlas never touches a network, and the orb's rim changes colour when the search you switched on is active, so you can always see it. Unplug the router and nothing about Atlas changes: that is the whole point of an offline AI assistant.

What machine do you need?

Atlas runs on the machine you already own — it was built and tested on a modest, fanless 8 GB MacBook Air. Honestly, by size:

8 GB — it works. Answers are slower and you will notice it thinking. It is still useful, every day.
16 GB — the sweet spot. Quick answers, long documents handled comfortably, voice without a wait.
32 GB+ — it flies. Big libraries, big models, everything at once.

Before you download, the Fit Check on the home page tells you straight what your computer can handle — if a machine can't run Atlas well, Atlas says that too. Editions today: Mac — available now · Linux — available now · Chromebook — available now · Windows — next. (One honest note we always make: many school-issued Chromebooks have their built-in Linux switched off by the school, and no app can change that. Personal Chromebooks are fine.)

The shelf that comes with it

Atlas doesn’t start empty, and it doesn’t stop growing. Its Library is a shelf of free Knowledge Packs — twenty in all, each one curated, sealed, and small enough to keep: the whole shelf is about 200 MB. The first, Everyday Knowledge, is downloadable now; the other nineteen are in development, and the Library page labels every one honestly. Packs come in depths — Essential, Standard, Comprehensive — so you take the shelf your disk can hold. And after the twenty, offline maps of your own region join the shelf: your state in your pocket, no signal required — also in development, and labelled so until the day it ships. None of it ever arrives by itself: Atlas never downloads behind your back.

An idea whose moment has arrived

The offline library is having a moment, and Atlas isn’t its only shape. Project NOMAD — a free, open-source project we admire — puts a whole library on a home server: the full Wikipedia, maps, courses and more, for the household that has a spare Linux machine to run it on. Atlas is the same idea for the laptop you already own: no server, no terminal — a window instead of a closet. We compared the two honestly, including the rows where NOMAD wins: Atlas vs Project NOMAD.

The honest trade

A tool that needs a datacentre will always be faster on paper. Atlas trades that speed for something no datacentre can offer — your notes never leave your desk. No subscription, no sign-up, no account, because there is no server to rent you. And because it arrives with a library of its own — free Knowledge Packs that work completely offline — the person with no files and no fast internet doesn't start empty.
