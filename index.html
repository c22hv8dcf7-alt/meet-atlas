Core belief

Software you own, that tells you the truth

Atlas exists because we believe the computer already on your desk is powerful enough — and that an assistant living on it should belong to you outright. Atlas is a free, sovereign, offline AI that lives locally on your hardware. It reads your library without watching you, remembers in files you own, cites where its answers came from, and tells you plainly what your machine can handle. Four promises hold the whole thing up:

◈

Zero cloud rent

No subscription, no sign-up, no account — and no background network requests. Nothing phones home, ever. The only network Atlas touches is the search you switch on, and the orb's rim changes colour when it's on, so you can always see it.

▤

Transparent memory

What Atlas remembers is written as plain, readable lines in a file on your own disk. Open it, read it, edit it, delete it — any time. No hidden profile, no black box, nothing about you stored anywhere you can't reach.

✓

Verifiable answers

Answers drawn from your library arrive with the source document attached, so you can check Atlas yourself instead of trusting it. And when an answer isn't in there, Atlas says so in plain words: "That's not in the library."

⚖

Honest hardware sizing

Atlas is built and tested on a modest machine — an 8 GB MacBook Air, fanless, everyday. Before you download, it tells you straight what your computer can handle and sizes itself to fit. If a machine can't run it well, Atlas says that too.

That's the whole pitch — and the tone never changes: no countdown timers, no fake scarcity, no bluffing. We state what Atlas does, state its limits, and let its own self-check numbers speak. Why the shelf exists

We found a flaw in our own idea — so we fixed it

Atlas began with a simple design: it reads your documents and answers from them, on your machine. For someone with a lifetime of files, that's beautiful. But then we wrote down who Atlas is really for — the student on a school Chromebook, the parent with no home internet, the worker whose whole digital life lives on a phone — and saw the flaw plainly: those people arrive with nothing to feed it. An assistant that knows nothing until you give it documents quietly serves the people who already have the most. That's the opposite of why Atlas exists. So we made a decision: Atlas would carry a library of its own, and hand it over free. Three reasons hold that decision up:

▤

Knowledge is already free

Encyclopedias, textbooks, first-aid manuals, farming and money guidance — humanity already wrote them and published them under open licences and public funding. What's actually rationed is access: bandwidth, subscriptions, discovery. The packs remove the rationing, not the knowledge.

✓

Chosen, not scraped

Every pack is curated from named sources, spot-checked, and signed before Atlas will trust it. Offline, there's no one to ask and nothing to Google — so the shelf has to be answerable-for before it ships, by us, by name. A library you can vouch for beats the whole internet dumped on a disk.

◈

Yours, the same way

Free forever, no account, works with the Wi-Fi off, delete any pack and the space is yours again. The shelf obeys the same laws as everything else in Atlas — because a gift with a meter on it isn't a gift, it's a trial.

We didn't add the packs to make Atlas bigger. We added them so the person with the least starts with the most already there. That's the whole reason — the shelf below is just the decision, kept.

Knowledge packs

It arrives already knowing things

An assistant that only reads your files has an empty shelf on day one — and the people who most need a free AI are often the ones with no files, no fast internet, and no subscription money. So Atlas doesn't start empty. Knowledge Packs are free bundles of real, sourced knowledge — an encyclopedia shelf, a school year's textbooks, a first-aid manual — that live on your machine and work completely offline. Download them once (or take them from a friend's USB stick) and they answer forever, cited like everything else Atlas says.

Every pack is built only from openly licensed and public sources, carries its sources inside it, and is signed — Atlas checks the signature before it trusts a single page, so a tampered pack gets refused in plain words. Packs are knowledge, never code: they can be read and cited, never obeyed. Delete one and the shelf space is yours again. This is the part we keep coming back to: for someone who can't afford the meter, this isn't a smaller AI. It's the first one that's actually there.

Our story

The Atlas story

In 2026, you paid rent in subscriptions and sign-ups, your questions travelled to a data centre, and the thing that knew your work best belonged to a company you’d never met.

Atlas began that fall, with one researcher and one small laptop — an 8 GB MacBook Air, the kind of machine people actually own — and a library of real research that deserved better than a stranger’s server.

Then it changed: it learned to be company, not just a research box — to live in the notch, a window, or the Room — and not to arrive empty. Knowledge Packs give Atlas a real library from the day it lands, and the Classroom, the Ledger, and the Darkroom give it tools to work with.

People who saw it asked the same question: can I have one? So it was rebuilt clean — everything personal taken out, everything honest kept in — and given away.

Under the hood

Built like it matters. Checked like it too.

“Private” is easy to say. These three are written into Atlas's build sheet for the public edition — the exact mechanisms, not the adjectives. In the build sheet · Items 18–20

🛡 0 KB out · Network quiet

The Shield

Atlas's network silence, made visible. A small teal badge lives in the corner: nothing has left this machine. Click it and you get the readable log — local activity only. If anything ever tries to phone out, the badge turns amber — amber means problem, here as everywhere in Atlas — and tells you plainly: “Atlas blocked an outgoing connection attempt.”

“Your memory file was modified outside of Atlas. Review the changes, or restore the backup?”

The Memory Guard

Your memory is a plain-text file you can open, read, and edit — and Atlas guards it like it matters. It's sealed with an integrity check: if anything changes it while Atlas is closed, Atlas asks before it trusts a single line. And memory is always treated as data, never instructions — a note can tell Atlas about your life; it can never give Atlas orders.

RAM headroom Comfortable

The Health Monitor

Fit Check doesn't retire after setup — it stays as a health drawer. Which model is running, how big, what the speed trade-off is, how much memory headroom you have, whether the index needs housekeeping. Nearing your machine's limit, Atlas says so before it stutters, and offers to tidy up instead of slowing down.

Also in the sheet, because a review board with engineers on it asked: changed files re-index alone (no stale answers from old copies), deleted files' embeddings are pruned, indexing always pauses while Atlas is answering, bundled programs and model files are hash-checked at launch, and the channels between Atlas and its own helpers are authenticated. Boring, invisible, load-bearing.

Before you ask

Three questions everyone asks

Is it really free? What’s the catch?

It’s really free: no account, no subscription, no API keys, no trial clock. The catch, if you want one, is that Atlas is a small independent project.

Does Atlas work completely offline?

Yes. The brain, voice, memory and your library all live on your Mac — a full week at a cabin, Wi-Fi off, everything working.

What Mac do I need?

An Apple Silicon Mac (M1 or later) with 8 GB of RAM or more. Atlas was built and is tested on an 8 GB MacBook Air — if it runs there, it runs on yours.
